How to compare the true cost of a used car

A low sticker price doesn't always mean a low-cost car. Before committing, compare the total amount you will spend to purchase, maintain, and operate it.

1. Calculate your out-the-door price

Start with the agreed sale price. Add sales tax, title and registration charges, and any dealer fees. Subtract your down payment to estimate how much you need to finance. Ask for a written itemized quote before signing.

2. Compare loan terms, not just monthly payments

A longer loan can lower your monthly payment while increasing the interest paid over time. Compare offers using the same amount financed and loan term. For a fixed-rate loan, monthly payment is calculated from principal, monthly interest rate, and number of payments. Remember to compare the total paid, not only the monthly figure.

3. Get an insurance quote before buying

Insurance premiums can vary significantly by vehicle, location, driver, and coverage. Request a quote for the exact vehicle and coverage you need before making a commitment.

4. Plan for repairs and maintenance

Review service records and check for recalls. Consider a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic. Set aside money for tires, brakes, fluids, unexpected repairs, and any known model-specific issues. An inspection reduces uncertainty but cannot guarantee future reliability.

5. Include fuel and other recurring costs

Estimate annual fuel cost by dividing yearly miles by expected miles per gallon, then multiplying by the price per gallon. Add parking, tolls, and recurring registration costs if applicable.

Example (illustrative only): A $12,000 vehicle with $1,500 in taxes and fees, $1,200 in annual insurance, $1,800 in fuel, and $1,000 in maintenance has a first-year cash cost of $17,500 before financing interest and other expenses. Actual costs will vary.

6. Compare two vehicles on the same basis

Write down each car's out-the-door cost, expected financing interest, annual insurance, fuel, maintenance reserve, and inspection findings. Compare the same ownership period and mileage. A more expensive vehicle can sometimes cost less overall if it is substantially cheaper to operate and maintain, but estimates are uncertain.
